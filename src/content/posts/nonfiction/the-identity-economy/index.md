<p>The Clean Internet Has a Dirty Secret</p>
<p>The average person spends hours a day on platforms designed to feel effortless. Scrolling feeds, ordering services, streaming media. What most users never see is that behind every content filter, algorithm, chatbot or safety system, there are people. And many of them are underpaid, outsourced, and psychologically unprotected.</p>
<p>Across the Global South, hundreds of thousands of digital workers, including content moderators and data annotators, sustain the modern tech ecosystem. They view and classify violent videos so your feed stays clean. They tag photos for AI to recognize faces. They label speech, flag scams, and sort hate speech in dozens of languages. Their labor is foundational, but they remain unseen by design.</p>
<p>This is not new. It is the digital continuation of a long-standing pattern: extractive economies built on invisible labor. Today, the work happens in front of a screen instead of a factory or field. But the logic of invisibility, disposability, and profit remains intact.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>The Digital Supply Chain Few People Talk About</p>
<p>The term “content moderation” sounds technical. In practice, it means spending hours reviewing footage of hate speech, suicide, murder, abuse, and harassment. It is relentless, graphic, and often traumatizing.</p>
<p>In the Philippines, an estimated 150,000 digital workers are engaged in online content review, tagging, and data annotation for companies including Meta, TikTok, and Google through outsourcing vendors (Fairwork, 2023). In Kenya, content moderators hired through Sama, a third-party vendor for Meta and OpenAI, were paid around $1.46 per hour to filter violent and disturbing content, often without adequate psychological support (Time, 2023).</p>
<p>In India, companies such as iMerit and TASL employ thousands of workers to label images, transcribe speech, or classify data used in AI model training. These tasks range from identifying traffic signs to annotating facial expressions. Workers are typically paid by task and are not classified as employees, which excludes them from basic labor protections.</p>
<p>This labor follows a predictable structure. Platforms outsource to third-party vendors who hire contract workers under strict non-disclosure agreements. This limits liability and reduces costs. Workers often have no direct contact with the platform whose product they support.</p>
<p>Tech platforms claim to be powered by innovation. In reality, much of their infrastructure is powered by outsourced, piecemeal labor. And often trauma.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>Why the Global South? Because It’s Cheap and Disposable</p>
<p>The question is not why this work exists, but why it is done where it is. The answer is as consistent as it is uncomfortable. It is cheaper to hire digital labor in the Global South, and easier to do it without accountability.</p>
<p>Countries like Kenya, India, and the Philippines offer a specific combination: large, educated populations fluent in English, low labor costs, expanding broadband access, and minimal labor regulation for outsourced service industries. These are the exact conditions global platforms seek when they build contracts with third-party firms.</p>
<p>A 2023 investigation by Time revealed that content moderators working for Sama in Kenya, on behalf of OpenAI, were paid around $1.32 to $2.00 per hour, despite handling some of the most traumatic tasks in the AI training process. Contracts included non-disclosure clauses and limits on legal action, even in cases of psychological harm or wrongful dismissal (Time, 2023).</p>
<p>In the Philippines, a global outsourcing hub for content moderation, major vendors such as TaskUs and Teleperformance hire thousands of workers on short-term contracts. These roles are essential for platforms like Facebook, YouTube, and TikTok. Yet workers often report a lack of adequate counseling, restricted sick leave, and no right to collective bargaining (Fairwork, 2023; Amnesty International, 2022).</p>
<p>India has become a center for data annotation, a critical part of training AI systems. Companies like iMerit handle massive image, speech, and sensor datasets for clients in self-driving car development, fintech, and facial recognition. Workers are typically paid per task with no employment benefits, and many work from informal locations like internet cafés or shared housing (Rest of World, 2022).</p>
<p>These systems are structured for opacity. Outsourcing adds layers between worker and platform. Legal jurisdictions shift responsibility. The platforms that depend on this labor rarely acknowledge the workers behind it.</p>
<p>These jobs are not unskilled. They are undervalued by design.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>Psychological Labor, Zero Protection</p>
<p>Content moderation is not just repetitive. It is traumatic. Workers are exposed to thousands of pieces of harmful content per shift. These include violent assaults, sexual exploitation, and graphic self-harm. Mental health services are rarely adequate or accessible.</p>
<p>A 2022 report by Amnesty International documented high levels of PTSD, anxiety, and depression among moderators in outsourced facilities. Many platforms use outsourcing to limit liability. As a result, moderators are treated as expendable contractors rather than protected employees (Amnesty, 2022).</p>
<p>The situation in Kenya, where Sama moderated content for OpenAI and Meta, drew international scrutiny. Workers reported censorship of internal dissent, rushed dismissal processes, and a lack of trauma care. In the Philippines, where thousands of moderators are hired by BPO firms, workers have begun to push back. They are demanding counseling access, contracts with appeal processes, and wage floors that account for psychological risk (Rest of World, 2022).</p>
<p>Without standards, digital workers remain structurally exposed. The trauma is built into the job, but not into the labor protections.</p>
<p>Informational safety is being secured by people who are not safe themselves.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>AI Isn’t Magic. It’s Micropaid, Manual, and Mined</p>
<p>For all the talk of machine intelligence, most AI systems today still rely on the manual labor of people, especially those working in low-income countries under contract, without visibility or rights.</p>
<p>From training self-driving cars to powering facial recognition, artificial intelligence is built on massive volumes of labeled data. That data does not come pre-structured. It must be tagged, annotated, and categorized by people. Millions of them.</p>
<p>In India, Sri Lanka, and the Philippines, workers manually tag traffic signs, crop images, flag anomalies in financial transactions, and classify medical imagery for AI startups and multinationals. The labor is time-consuming and repetitive. Most of it is paid by the microtask, often just a few cents for every image labeled, phrase translated, or audio file segmented (Rest of World, 2022; Fairwork, 2023).</p>
<p>These workers often do not know what their work is for. In many cases, contracts prohibit disclosure of project details. A worker may spend hours tagging thousands of images of roads and never be told they are training the computer vision for a driverless vehicle being tested on another continent.</p>
<p>A 2023 analysis by the World Economic Forum estimated that over 85 percent of labeled data used in AI models is generated by human annotators, most of whom work in the Global South under subcontracting chains.</p>
<p>This is not a tech story. It is a labor story. A human workforce sustains the automation economy without credit, protection, or recognition.</p>
<p>Every “smart” system is powered by human hands somewhere.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>From Invisibility to Agency</p>
<p>Digital work is often framed as the future. But the workers doing that labor are still stuck in the past, without contracts, protections, or recourse. That is not because these roles are too new to regulate. It is because regulation has not kept up with the scale and structure of outsourced digital labor.</p>
<p>Across the Global South, efforts to change that are underway. Some focus on transparency. Others push for pay equity, legal protection, and mental health safeguards.</p>
<p>In India and Kenya, researchers and advocates from the Fairwork Project have developed ratings that assess tech platforms based on five principles: fair pay, fair conditions, fair contracts, fair management, and fair representation. These public scorecards have helped expose poor labor practices in digital gig work and pressured companies to adopt improvements. In 2023, not one company studied in either country scored above 5 out of 10 (Fairwork, 2023).</p>
<p>In the Philippines, workers at content moderation firms like TaskUs have begun to organize around mental health rights and contract reform. With support from local labor organizations, they are pushing for trauma counseling, transparent grievance processes, and wage floors that account for psychological risk (Rest of World, 2022).</p>
<p>Policy is beginning to respond. The International Labour Organization and UNESCO have called for platform companies to be held accountable for worker conditions across their supply chains. This includes contract transparency, rights to collective bargaining, and the extension of occupational health protections to digital labor roles (ILO, 2023; UNESCO, 2022).</p>
<p>Some experts argue for a new kind of audit, not just of algorithms or datasets, but of the labor behind them. Algorithmic accountability must include labor visibility. If a platform needs human moderators or annotators to function, those workers should be treated as part of the core workforce, not as anonymous freelancers hidden behind outsourcing chains.</p>
<p>The future of tech cannot be ethical if the labor behind it remains invisible.</p>

<p>What Would Change If We Counted the Hands Behind the Code?</p>
<p>Who gets to be seen as a tech worker?</p>
<p>What kind of economy builds intelligence on labor it refuses to name?</p>
<p>And what does it say about the future of innovation if it depends on people nobody protects?</p>
<p>AI is not clean. It is not self-sufficient. It is not labor-free.</p>
<p>It is time we stop pretending the algorithm works on its own.</p>
