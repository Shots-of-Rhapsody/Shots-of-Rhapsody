<p>The Workers Who Keep the System Running, Without Being Counted</p>
<p>More than 60 percent of the world’s workers do not exist on official payrolls. They have no formal contracts, no benefits, and no legal protections. They are the street vendors, construction day laborers, home-based producers, and domestic workers who make cities function but are rarely seen in economic data or policy debates.</p>
<p>In much of the Global South, this kind of work is not marginal. It is foundational.</p>
<p>Yet national budgets, recovery plans, and growth models continue to focus almost exclusively on the formal sector, treating informal labor as temporary, inefficient, or invisible. The assumption is that with enough investment and reform, informality will eventually disappear, replaced by salaried jobs, office towers, and a modern workforce.</p>
<p>But that has not happened. And it is not a coincidence.</p>
<p>The informal economy has not been left behind. It has been structured out. Its exclusion is not evidence of failure. It is evidence of a system designed to extract without acknowledging the people it relies on.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>Where Work Happens, But Doesn’t Count</p>
<p>Informal labor is defined by what it lacks. It does not involve legal contracts, social protections, stable wages, or consistent regulatory oversight. But what it contributes is undeniable. Informal workers make up the majority of the workforce in many of the world’s largest economies. They provide essential goods and services in sectors such as construction, transport, food, waste collection, domestic work, and home-based manufacturing.</p>
<p>In India, more than 90 percent of the workforce is employed informally, according to both the International Labour Organization and the National Sample Survey Office (ILO, 2023; NSSO, Periodic Labour Force Survey, 2020). In Kenya, the informal sector accounts for over 80 percent of total employment (Kenya National Bureau of Statistics, 2023). In Indonesia, just over 57 percent of workers are informally employed, especially in agriculture, retail, and transport sectors (BPS Indonesia, 2023).</p>
<p>These numbers are not statistical noise. They reflect a structural reality. In much of the Global South, the majority of work does not happen in offices, factories, or legally registered companies. It takes place in open-air markets, private homes, side streets, and shifting urban spaces, often at wages below the legal minimum and without recourse when payments are delayed or denied.</p>
<p>What unites these workers is not what they do, but how systems fail to recognize them. Without legal status, informal workers are excluded from pension schemes, labor courts, unemployment insurance, and workplace safety enforcement. In some countries, they are even excluded from national ID systems, which further blocks access to healthcare, schooling, or financial services.</p>
<p>The informal majority does not exist because the system is broken. It exists because the system was never built to include them.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>Why Systems Prefer the Invisible</p>
<p>Informality is often described as a transitional phase, a stepping stone on the path to modern development. The assumption is that, over time, informal workers will gradually move into formal employment as economies grow and institutions mature.</p>
<p>But this assumption does not hold up to global evidence. In many countries, the share of informal workers is not declining. It is holding steady or growing.</p>
<p>According to the International Labour Organization, informality remains the dominant employment form in Africa, South Asia, and parts of Southeast Asia, even in countries experiencing economic growth and rising investment. In sub-Saharan Africa, 89 percent of workers operate informally. In South Asia, the figure exceeds 85 percent (ILO, 2023).</p>
<p>This persistence is not a policy failure. It is the result of how modern economies have been designed.</p>
<p>Informal labor benefits governments by offering labor market flexibility without requiring the fiscal responsibility of benefits, enforcement, or infrastructure. Employers benefit as well. They can avoid minimum wage laws, worker protections, and social contributions, while keeping labor costs low and compliance risk minimal.</p>
<p>Municipal authorities often tax or license informal workers such as market vendors or transport operators without offering sanitation, healthcare, or safety services in return.</p>
<p>In many countries, international lenders and institutions have promoted deregulation and fiscal tightening. These reforms may encourage short-term economic efficiency, but they also incentivize labor informality by loosening worker protections and reducing formal employment incentives (World Bank, 2020).</p>
<p>In export-oriented sectors, large brands reduce costs by outsourcing to informal producers. A 2022 report by Oxfam found that home-based women garment workers in Dhaka earned less than half the legal minimum wage and were entirely excluded from formal supply chain accountability, despite producing for global retail brands (Oxfam Bangladesh, 2022).</p>
<p>Informal labor is not temporary or exceptional. It is a durable, engineered system that delivers flexibility and low cost to those in power while denying security and recognition to the people who work within it.</p>
<p>Exclusion is not a side effect. It is part of the operating model.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>Fragility by Design</p>
<p>When crisis hits, the informal economy does not bend. It breaks.</p>
<p>Informal workers, by definition, operate without contracts, social protections, or access to institutional safety nets. This makes them vulnerable not only to economic shocks, but also to the everyday volatility of life such as illness, injury, inflation, climate stress, or sudden displacement. When systemic disruptions occur, informal workers are usually the first to fall and the last to recover.</p>
<p>The COVID-19 pandemic made this visible across the world.</p>
<p>In Nigeria, a survey by Women in Informal Employment: Globalizing and Organizing (WIEGO) found that 80 percent of urban informal workers lost all or most of their income within the first month of lockdowns (WIEGO, 2020). In India, the Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy (CMIE) estimated that over 100 million informal and migrant workers lost employment during the first wave, with many walking or cycling hundreds of kilometers to return to rural villages (CMIE, 2020; ILO, 2021).</p>
<p>Unlike formal employees, most informal workers were ineligible for government stimulus, wage subsidies, or loan deferment schemes. Many lacked the legal ID, bank accounts, or digital access required to apply for support.</p>
<p>Women were disproportionately affected. In sectors like domestic work, vending, and home-based production, informal female workers were abruptly cut off from income and excluded from digital commerce transitions. A 2021 WIEGO multi-country study found that female informal workers experienced deeper income loss and slower recovery than their male counterparts across Africa, Asia, and Latin America (WIEGO, 2021).</p>
<p>These outcomes were not incidental. They revealed what happens when labor is needed but never protected.</p>
<p>Informality is often framed as flexibility. But in practice, it is a system built on disposability.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>From Recognition to Protection</p>
<p>The informal economy is not going away. It is not a developmental glitch or a temporary stage to be outgrown. It is a persistent, deeply embedded part of global economic life. If anything, new forms of informality are emerging in platform work, subcontracted gig labor, and cross-border digital services.</p>
<p>The path forward is not to force formality for its own sake. It is to design systems that include what already exists.</p>
<p>That begins with legal and institutional recognition. Without recognition, there is no path to protection. Informal workers need to be counted in labor statistics, consulted in policy processes, and included in recovery plans. Governments and employers cannot continue to benefit from their labor while excluding them from rights.</p>
<p>Some frameworks already exist.</p>
<p>The International Labour Organization’s Recommendation 204 provides a roadmap for transitioning informal work into protected employment without requiring formal registration as a first step. It calls for policies that extend rights, protections, and access to social insurance regardless of employment classification (ILO, 2015).</p>
<p>Local efforts also show what is possible. In India, the Self-Employed Women’s Association (SEWA) has organized over two million women across sectors like street vending, home-based work, and waste picking. Through collective bargaining, micro-insurance, and co-operatives, SEWA has built informal structures where formal ones failed.</p>
<p>In Kenya, matatu associations have created self-managed savings schemes and negotiated with municipal authorities to gain recognition and infrastructure access. In Indonesia, urban vendor groups have used participatory planning to influence zoning laws and push back against displacement.</p>
<p>Social protection systems must also evolve. Countries like Thailand and Uruguay have piloted inclusive pension and health insurance models that decouple benefits from employment contracts. These models show that protection can be universal, not just job-based.</p>
<p>The point is not to romanticize informality. It is to reject the false binary between modern and invisible. Informal work is already part of the economy. The only question is whether systems will finally reflect that reality.</p>
<p>An economy is not inclusive if it depends on workers it refuses to protect.</p>
<p>⸻</p>
<p>What Happens If We Change What Counts?</p>
<p>What if the majority of workers are not outside the system, but excluded by design?</p>
<p>What do we call an economy that relies on people it refuses to protect?</p>
<p>What would change if we started measuring what already exists instead of only what fits?</p>
<p>It is time to stop calling informality a gap and start recognizing it as the structure.</p>
<p>If we want a just economy, we have to begin by changing what counts.</p>
